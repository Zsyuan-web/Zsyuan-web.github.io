It's 3:17 AM. You've checked your phone five times. You have a presentation at nine, the deck isn't finished, and your heartbeat is playing through your eardrums. Your brain is screening a disaster film — you freeze up, the room goes quiet, your boss frowns. Each version more vivid than the last.

You've tried deep breathing. You've tried counting sheep. You've tried telling yourself "just stop thinking." Nothing works.

Because the whole time, you've been asking the same question: **How do I stop being anxious?**

The question itself is built on a false premise.

---

Anxiety rests on two assumptions. You've never checked either one.

First, **you believe the future is in your hands**. If the future were completely out of your control, you wouldn't be anxious — you'd be resigned. Anxiety lives in the crack between "I can still do something" and "I'm not sure it's enough." So you prepare, rehearse, simulate.

But here's the thing: the effort you're using to "control the future" — what controls *that*? Whether you can focus on your deck tonight depends on how well you slept last night. How well you slept depends on that afternoon coffee. Whether you had that coffee depends on your coworker asking you to grab one at three. You can't find a starting point where "you" are in charge.

Nāgārjuna said the same thing eighteen hundred years ago:

> No thing arises from itself, nor from another, nor from both, nor without a cause. — *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā*

Translation for 3 AM: every moment is piled up from countless conditions. There's no control room. Nobody's at the wheel. It's not that you're failing to control things — it's that "control" as a concept doesn't hold up.

Second, **you believe there's a "you" being threatened**.

If the presentation bombs — who looks bad? You. Who gets judged? You.

Who is that "you"? Take it apart and look. Your body, your sensations, your perceptions, your impulses, your consciousness — the five aggregates — are swapping out every second. Ten seconds ago you were anxious about the deck. Nine seconds ago you remembered your ex saying you weren't good enough. Eight seconds ago you got hungry. Which one of those is "you"?

> Form is impermanent. What is impermanent is suffering. What is suffering is not-self. — *Saṃyuktāgama*

A thing made of constantly swapping parts, worrying about another thing's constantly changing future. The subject is in motion. The object is in motion. Anxiety is a castle built on a river.

The feeling is real. Your heart is actually racing. Your palms are actually sweating. But the logical foundation under it — paper-thin.

---

Zhuangzi tells a story. A man is crossing a river. An empty boat drifts into his. He doesn't get angry — there's nobody in the boat. But if there's a person standing in that boat, he starts shouting, cursing, waving his arms.

Same collision. Same force. The extra layer is something *you* added: the belief that someone is steering.

Anxiety works the same way. Uncertainty is just uncertainty. You're the one translating it into "this is aimed at me," "this is a sign things will go wrong," "I'm definitely going to blow it." That layer of narrative — you built it yourself.

---

Notice something? Neither tradition prescribes "stop being anxious." They simply disassemble the premises.

Control is an illusion. The self isn't fixed. Uncertainty doesn't need an enemy.

They leave one question unanswered: if all those premises collapse — what about the thing that's still asking "what do I do?"

You might not sleep tonight. That might not be the point.
